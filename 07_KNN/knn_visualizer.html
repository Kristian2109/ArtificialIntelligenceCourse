How to read these examples

The points are synthetic two-moons data. Every graph shares the same 100 training examples. The noise slider flips that percentage of training labels; the 240 independent validation points retain their original labels. Changing noise keeps the feature positions and validation set fixed. New sample regenerates both sets.

Training accuracy includes each point itself, so k = 1 gets 100% training accuracy on these distinct points even when labels are corrupted. The validation curve shows how well those choices generalize. Which k works best depends on the sample; validation is for tuning, and a real project should reserve a separate test set.

The metric graphs emphasize the nine voters without connection lines or decision backgrounds. Their contours are computed in feature coordinates, then fitted to the plot axes; the displayed aspect ratio can stretch their shapes. A different metric can change the selected points without necessarily changing the predicted class.

All votes have equal weight. Class-vote ties use the nearest point's label; equal distances are ordered by point ID. Small k does not always overfit, and large k does not always underfit. These examples show common effects, rather than a universal best setting.
